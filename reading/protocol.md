# Reading as a writer

You already read technical work for content. This protocol is for reading it for *craft*: seeing the decisions the author made so you can make them on purpose. Budget 45 minutes a week. Keep notes in `journal/` with `python tools/tw.py new reading SLUG`, or in a plain file.

## The protocol

1. **First pass, for content (10 min).** Read normally. At the end, write the author's main claim in one sentence, from memory.
2. **Check (2 min).** Find the sentence where the author states that claim. Where is it: title, first paragraph, end of the introduction, or nowhere? Did your sentence match theirs?
3. **Reverse outline (15 min).** One line per paragraph (per section for a long paper): what does it *do*? Use verbs: *claims, motivates, defines, shows evidence, concedes, compares, generalises*. This is the author's structure laid bare.
4. **List the moves (10 min).** Note three to five moves worth stealing. A move is a reusable technique, such as:
   - "Opens with a measurement that makes the problem feel urgent."
   - "States the result before the method."
   - "Concedes the strongest objection in its own paragraph, then answers it."
   - "Every figure caption is a full sentence stating the takeaway."
   - "Defines each symbol in the sentence that introduces it."
5. **Find one weakness (5 min).** Where did you get lost, skim or doubt? Exemplars have faults too. Seeing them shows that the rules are about the reader, not about authority.
6. **Steal one move (3 min).** Write which move you will use in this week's piece, and where.

## What to notice, by form

| Form | Look for |
|---|---|
| Blog | The first 150 words; how code and prose alternate; how the post ends |
| White paper | The executive summary on its own; how options are compared; where cost appears |
| Paper | The abstract's five moves; the contributions list; how evaluation questions are framed; how related work positions the paper |
| Review / rebuttal | Which comments the authors addressed with new evidence, which with argument; tone |

## Imitation, once

Once in the twelve weeks, try the classic exercise of writers from Benjamin Franklin onwards. Take one paragraph you admire, note its moves in a list, close the source, and write a paragraph *on your own topic* that makes the same moves. Then compare. You are copying the structure, never the words.
