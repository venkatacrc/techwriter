# 8. Revision

Drafting is getting the material out of your head; revision is making it usable by someone else's. Professionals spend more time revising than drafting. Revise in passes, from big to small: fixing sentences in a section you later delete wastes the work.

## The passes, in order

| Pass | Question | Tool |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Argument | Is the claim right-sized and fully supported? | Claim–evidence table (D12) |
| 2. Structure | Are the points in the reader's order, one per paragraph? | Reverse outline (D07) |
| 3. Paragraphs | Does each first sentence state the point? | Read first sentences only |
| 4. Sentences | Actors as subjects, old before new, subject near verb? | `principles/03`, read aloud (D44) |
| 5. Words | Filler, hedges, vague claims, undefined terms? | `tw lint` |
| 6. Evidence details | Units, baselines, conditions, spread on every number? | `principles/04` checklist |
| 7. Figures | One message each; captions state it? | `principles/06` |
| 8. Mechanics | Spelling, references, figure numbers, links, formatting | Spell-checker, a final read |

## Get distance

You cannot see your own text while you still remember writing it. Create distance:

- **Cool off.** Wait at least 24 hours before reviewing (`tw review` enforces it).
- **Change the medium.** Print it, or read it on your phone, or in a different font.
- **Read it aloud.** You will stumble exactly where a reader would.
- **Read it as your named reader.** At each paragraph ask: would Priya know this word? Would she ask "compared with what?" here?

## Cut

Most first drafts are 20–30% longer than they need to be. Cut in this order: sections the claim does not need, paragraphs that repeat, sentences that announce what the next sentence says ("In this section we will discuss..."), and words that do no work. Drill D43 makes this a habit. Cutting never removes a claim or a number; if it does, you cut the wrong thing.

## Using feedback

- **Ask specific questions.** "Is it clear?" gets "yes". "Where did you stop reading? What do you think the main claim is? Which number did you not trust?" gets data.
- **A reader's confusion is evidence; their suggested fix is a hypothesis.** If a reader stumbled at paragraph 4, something is wrong at or before paragraph 4. Their proposed rewrite may not be the right repair.
- **Track changes you will make**, in the review sheet's last column, and make them in one later session.
- **Thank reviewers, and tell them what you changed.** It is how you get reviewed again.

## Using AI tools

Use language models as reviewers, not writers. `prompts/reviewer.md` asks a model to quote your sentences and name problems without rewriting them. Three reasons:

1. **Learning.** If a model rewrites, the model improves and you do not. The muscle you are building is the rewrite.
2. **Accuracy.** Models produce fluent text that can be subtly wrong about your system, and fluent wrongness is the hardest kind to catch in review.
3. **Policy and voice.** Many journals and conferences restrict AI-generated text and require disclosure of AI assistance; check the venue's policy before you submit. Editors and readers also recognise generic model prose, and it costs you credibility.

## When is it done?

A piece is done when all of these are true:

- The claim, reader and evidence lines at the top of the draft are true of the text.
- The reverse outline is in the reader's order.
- `tw lint` shows no unexplained *fix* items.
- Every number has its five parts, traceable to a log.
- One real reader has read it, and their confusion has been addressed.

Then ship it. Polishing past this point has fast-diminishing returns, and the next piece will teach you more.
